The Launch Day Video

What has to be true about your product before a launch video is worth making at all.

We showed up expecting a fairly standard launch-day shoot: a founder walking through a demo, some hero shots, maybe a rooftop toast at the end. What we got instead, at HLOS's "Et Tu, Claude" event this March at STAK in Oakland, was a room full of AI agent builders invited to actively attack the company's own live settlement infrastructure — on camera, with no dress rehearsal.

Twenty-seven minutes

That's roughly how long it took the room to find the seam between the sandbox and production. From there: 424 adversarial accounts, 109 Stripe checkout sessions initiated, close to $60,000 in attempted purchases — and zero dollars actually lost. As a founder watching from behind a camera, that gap between "attempted" and "lost" is the only number that matters. It's the difference between a vulnerability and an incident.

What's harder to fake on camera is what happened next: multiple independent AI agents converged on overlapping holes faster than any single team could have triaged them by hand. That's not a story about one clever exploit. It's a preview of how fast automated red-teaming is starting to move, for anyone building payment or settlement infrastructure right now.

The clearest example of that on camera was Michael Canniffe, who finished sixth. His first idea — routing money back through a fake internal service — didn't hold up, so he switched approach mid-event and chained a series of smaller gaps into a full compromise of the application layer. The lesson he pulled out of it is the one that stuck with us afterward: AI-written code tends to default to permissive, completing a request before it's actually confirmed who's allowed to make it, unless someone deliberately builds it to fail closed.

The part that actually earns trust

The core cryptographic receipt layer held. The authentication scaffolding around it didn't — and when it came up, HLOS's founder didn't hedge on camera: "that part is on me." Most companies bury the honest version of what broke under a press-friendly one. Being willing to let a camera into the room for that conversation is what made this worth filming in the first place.

The island

The reflection that stuck with us most as filmmakers wasn't about the exploits at all — it was a section Andrew wrote afterward that he called the island. Spend long enough building inside your own assumptions, he argued, and overconfidence quietly turns into miscalibration: a team can start mistaking motion for progress once nothing from outside is pushing back on it. Events like this one exist to force that outside contact back into the loop. As he put it, "contact with reality is the only way to build systems that deserve trust."

What's next

Andrew closed by laying out where HLOS goes from here: splitting future events into two separate tracks instead of one. The adversarial experiments stay invite-level and open-ended, built for people who already know how to break things. Alongside them, a new track of structured, beginner-focused workshops gives newer builders an actual way in — though nobody's turned away from the harder track just for being new to it.

We've made launch-day videos for a few dozen founders since Houdy's got started, and the range keeps surprising us — pre-seed teams shooting in a spare bedroom on one end, teams already backed by firms like Y Combinator and a16z on the other. A handful of the people we've worked with came straight out of teams at Stripe, Notion, and Ramp before going out on their own. If your launch is actually ready and you want it filmed honestly instead of oversold, reach out.

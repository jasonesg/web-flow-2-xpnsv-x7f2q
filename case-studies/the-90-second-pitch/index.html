The 90-Second Pitch Video

What actually earns a founder a second meeting with an investor — and it's rarely the drone shot.

Alex Ayala · San Francisco

Every founder who comes to us with a "pitch video" ask starts by describing something cinematic. Sweeping shots of the office. A moody voiceover. Maybe a drone, if the budget stretches. We understand the instinct — it's what a "professional" video is supposed to look like.

Almost none of it is what gets watched past the first fifteen seconds.

What an investor is actually doing while it plays

They're skimming. They've got a stack of decks and a calendar full of intro calls, and your video is one of a dozen tabs open on their second monitor. The video's job isn't to impress them with production value — it's to survive being skimmed and still leave them with one clear, memorable claim about why this company is worth thirty more minutes of their week.

That means cutting almost everything a "pitch video" traditionally includes:

No cold open with ambient b-roll of the skyline. No slow-build voiceover setting a mood before you say what the company does. Say what it does, who it's for, and why now — in the first ten seconds, on camera, in the founder's own words.

"Clarity is the production value that actually matters here."

Why we shoot the founder talking, not a montage

Numbers and traction slides can go in the deck. What a video uniquely does is let an investor hear conviction in a person's voice — the thing that's genuinely hard to fake and genuinely easy to sense the absence of. So we build these around one thing: the founder, on camera, explaining the company like they're explaining it to a smart friend who's never heard of it.

We keep it to 90 seconds because that's roughly the length where "concise" tips into "thorough enough." Past that, you're relying on someone's patience instead of their interest.

The part nobody wants to hear

The best pitch videos we've made took less time to shoot than the deck took to write. One camera, one good mic, one take that doesn't sound rehearsed. If you're spending more on the video than you're raising in the round it's meant to support, something's gone sideways.
